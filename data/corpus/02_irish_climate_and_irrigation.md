# Irish climate and why irrigation is a marginal but growing question

## The maritime baseline

Ireland has a temperate maritime climate: mild winters, cool summers, and
rainfall distributed across the whole year rather than concentrated in a wet
season. Annual rainfall is strongly west-east: western and north-western
counties typically receive well over 1,000 mm a year, while parts of the east
and south-east receive appreciably less. Met Éireann publishes the authoritative
long-term averages by station.

Because rainfall is frequent and evaporative demand is low compared with
southern Europe, most Irish tillage and grassland is rain-fed. Irrigation
infrastructure is uncommon outside horticulture, soft fruit, and some
high-value vegetable production.

## Why the question is changing

Three pressures make irrigation scheduling relevant even in a wet country:

1. **Dry spells within a wet year.** An annual rainfall total says nothing
   about a three-week rainless period in June, which is exactly when spring
   crops are at peak demand. Season totals hide the risk.
2. **Notable drought years.** Summer 2018 produced a prolonged dry period
   across northern Europe, with significant grass growth and fodder deficits in
   Ireland, and brought water restrictions.
3. **Seasonal redistribution.** The pattern of concern is wetter winters and
   drier growing-season months, which moves rainfall away from the period when
   crops actually need it.

## The practical decision

For an Irish grower, the decision is rarely "how much water should I apply all
season". It is a daily or weekly binary: **irrigate today, or wait for the
forecast rain**. Getting that wrong is expensive in both directions.

- Irrigating unnecessarily wastes water, energy, and labour, and can leach
  nutrients below the root zone.
- Failing to irrigate through a short dry spell at a sensitive growth stage
  costs yield that cannot be recovered later.

## Why a sensor-free approach matters here

Soil moisture probe networks are expensive to buy, install and maintain, and
the economics are hard to justify on a small holding where irrigation is used
only a few weeks a year. A method that uses freely available weather
observations, published agronomic coefficients and a model trained on
historical patterns lowers the barrier to entry, at the cost of being less
precise than a well-calibrated probe.
